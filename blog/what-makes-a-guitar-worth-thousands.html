What actually makes a guitar worth thousands?

EM
Elias MarrFounder & luthier · September 18, 2026

It’s a fair question. A factory guitar can cost a few hundred dollars, so what justifies a price in the thousands? The honest answer is time, attention, and the number of decisions a person makes on your behalf. A logo doesn’t come into it.

Wood that was chosen, not just cut

Production guitars are cut from whatever lumber arrives that week, to a grade. A hand-built instrument starts with a luthier weighing, tap-testing and checking individual boards for grain and runout, and rejecting far more than they accept. The rejects are part of what you pay for.

A neck that was given time

The neck is the part you touch and the part most likely to move. Premium builders use more stable materials and methods such as roasted maple, quartersawn laminates and rested blanks, and they hand-carve the profile to the player. We describe our own approach in our guide to roasted maple necks.

Hand carving vs. CNC-only production

CNC machines are superb for accurate rough shapes, and we use one. What a machine can’t do is feel the wood. The final carve of the neck profile, heel and top is shaped and checked by hand until it’s right, not merely within tolerance.

Fretwork you can feel

Factory fretwork is fast and good enough. Hand-levelled, hand-crowned and polished stainless frets with rolled ends are the difference between “it plays fine” and a neck that feels like glass, with low action and no buzz anywhere on the board.

Finish is more than paint

Thin, hand-sprayed finishes take longer, and nitro takes weeks of curing. A thinner finish feels more like wood under the hand and wears in honestly. (We compare the options in nitro vs. poly.)

One builder, start to finish

On a production line, a guitar passes through dozens of stations and dozens of hands. On a true custom build, one luthier takes it from rough wood to final setup, so one person is accountable for how it plays, not just how it looks. At Obsidian that person also signs the build sheet.

Paperwork and accountability

A serial number, a signed build sheet and a record of where the wood came from don’t change how a guitar sounds. They do change what it’s worth to a second owner, and they show the builder is willing to be held to their work.

The bottom line

You’re paying for the hours of selection, carving, fretwork, finishing and setup that go into an instrument meant to be played for decades. For a line-by-line breakdown, read how a custom build works and what it costs.
