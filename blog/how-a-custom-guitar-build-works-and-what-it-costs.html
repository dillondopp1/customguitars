How a custom guitar build works, and what it really costs

EM
Elias MarrFounder & luthier · September 29, 2026

If you’ve never commissioned a guitar, the process can feel opaque. Every builder words it differently, prices are often “on request”, and nobody explains why the deposit is the size it is. This is the guide we wish more shops published, including ours.

The short answer on cost

Hand-built electric guitars from small independent shops usually start somewhere between $3,000 and $5,000 and climb from there. Exotic woods, elaborate inlay and extended-range builds can easily pass $10,000. Custom shop instruments from the large brands sit in a similar band. Our own base prices run from $4,800 (Tephra) to $6,400 (Scoria), and most finished builds land between $5,500 and $8,000 once you’ve chosen a wood grade, finish and hardware.

A price is only meaningful when you know what’s in it, so here’s the process first and the money after.

How a custom build works, step by step

  1. The request. You describe what you play and what you want. A good builder will ask more about your hands and your music than about your budget.
  2. The spec conversation. Neck shape, scale length, radius, fret size, pickups, weight targets. This is where a builder earns their fee. They translate “I want it to feel fast” into millimetres.
  3. Written spec and quote. Every dimension and material in writing, with a fixed price. If a builder won’t put a price in writing before taking money, walk away.
  4. Deposit and slot. The deposit reserves time in the builder’s calendar and pays for materials bought specifically for you.
  5. The build. Wood prep, neck and body shaping, fretwork, finishing, assembly, wiring, setup. Expect progress photos; ask for them if they aren’t offered.
  6. Final setup and balance. The guitar is set up to your preferences, inspected, then the balance is paid and the instrument ships, insured.

How long it takes

Bench time for a hand-built guitar is commonly somewhere between 60 and 120 hours. It isn’t continuous, though. Glue needs to cure, wood needs to settle, and lacquer finishes in particular need weeks to harden before they can be buffed. That’s why calendar time is measured in months: anywhere from 8 weeks to more than a year depending on the builder’s queue. Ours is typically 12–16 weeks from deposit, with 12 slots a quarter.

Where the money goes in a $6,000 build

Every shop’s numbers differ. This is an illustrative breakdown for a mid-spec build at a small shop like ours, so you can see the shape of it:

Bench labour (roughly 80–100 hours)40–50%
Wood, including the boards we reject8–12%
Hardware, pickups, electronics, case12–18%
Finishing materials and booth time5–8%
Workshop, tooling, insurance, payment fees10–15%
Warranty reserve and profitthe remainder

The pattern holds across most honest builders: labour is the biggest line, and wood is a smaller share than most people expect. That’s why figured tops and premium finishes are priced as upgrades. They add hours as much as materials.

Deposits: what’s normal

Deposits of 25–50% are common. What matters more than the percentage is when it becomes non-refundable. A fair policy ties that to the point where the builder has actually committed resources to you, such as cutting your wood. Ours reads:

A 30% deposit reserves your build slot once you approve your written spec and fixed quote. It is fully refundable until your wood is cut, less the cost of any parts already ordered for your build; once construction begins it is non-refundable. The remaining 70% is due when your guitar passes final inspection, before it ships.

Questions to ask any builder

  • Who will physically build my guitar, and will they be the same person throughout?
  • Will I get a written spec and a fixed price before paying anything?
  • What happens if you miss the delivery date?
  • What documentation ships with the instrument: serial, build sheet, wood source?
  • What does the warranty cover, and does it transfer to a second owner?
  • Can I see progress photos during the build?

Red flags

  • Full payment up front, or a deposit with no written spec.
  • Vague timelines with no target month.
  • Endorsement-heavy marketing with no build detail.
  • Reluctance to say who actually does the work.

The bottom line

A custom guitar costs what it does because someone spends a hundred hours making decisions on your behalf. You should get to see those decisions in writing before you pay, and on paper when the guitar arrives.
